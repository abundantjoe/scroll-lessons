# PHerc.Paris. 4, final columns: physical, layout and imaging extract

Source: F. Nicolardi, D. Delattre, G. Del Mastro, R. Fowler, R. Janko, "The final columns of PHerc.Paris. 4 revealed through virtual unwrapping", Cronache Ercolanesi 54 (2024), pp. 9-27 (ISSN 0391-1535).

**How the PDF maps to the journal.** The PDF has 26 pages, and all were read.
- PDF pp. 1-6: cover, front matter and contents.
- PDF pp. 7-25: journal pp. 9-27, the article.
- PDF p. 26: blank.

The article has no separate bibliography; all references are in footnotes nn. 1-17 plus an unnumbered first note on p. 9. It is organised as follows:
- §1.1, physical features (pp. 9-11)
- §1.2, content (pp. 11-12; not extracted, per the restrictions)
- §1.3, the images (pp. 12-15)
- §2, the edition (pp. 15-25; only line numbers, lacuna signs, marks and layout notes were used)
- Figs. 8-9 (pp. 26-27)

**Tags used below**
- **[paper]**: stated by the authors.
- **[edition]**: derived from the printed edition's line numbering, lacuna signs or critical signs.
- **[fig-measure]**: my own measurement from the published figures. The authors do not state it.
- **[derived]**: my arithmetic from the paper's numbers.

For every [fig-measure] value, the scale comes from a 200-dpi render of Fig. 8 (journal p. 26). The paper's 79 mm column pitch corresponds to ~260 px and its 11.37 cm maximum height to ~397 px, so the scale is about 3.3-3.5 px/mm. As a check, the three row widths add up to ~4,090 px, about 1.17-1.24 m. That agrees with the paper's "around 1.2 m" strip.

## A. Where the final columns sit in the roll

### What the paper states
- **Part of the roll.** The strip holds "portions of the last sixteen columns of the roll", about 1.2 m long, from "the final part of the roll" (p. 9 abstract; p. 10) [paper].
- **Column numbering.** Columns are numbered negatively from -16 to -1, and col. -1 is "the last one". The authors expect "the preceding columns of the roll" to be found soon (p. 10 n. 4) [paper].
- **Left end of the strip.** Of col. -16 "only a few final letters of seven lines are visible". Cols. -15 to -1 are complete in width (p. 10) [paper]. The left end of the segmented strip therefore cuts through col. -16.
- **Total number of columns.** In passing (p. 12), the authors put the roll at "approximately one hundred and sixty" columns. They say "about two-thirds of fifteen columns" can currently be read [paper].
  - [derived] The 16 revealed columns are therefore roughly the last 10% of the roll.
  - [derived] 160 columns × 79 mm is about 12.6 m of written papyrus. This length is not stated in the paper.
- **Height of the roll.**
  - Segmentation has revealed up to 18 consecutive lines per column, with a maximum height of 11.37 cm.
  - The roll's total height is 16.75 cm, so 5.38 cm remain unrevealed "in the lower portion of the roll" (p. 10) [paper].
  - Segmentation has reached the upper edge of the papyrus, and the upper margin is lost (p. 10) [paper].
- **How the measurements were taken.** Stephen Parsons of the technical team, and Ben Kyles and David Josey of the Segmentation Team, took the measurements. They are based on the scan data of the "crumpled and shrunken" unopened roll. The original measurements, especially the height, "may be slightly bigger" (p. 10 n. 5) [paper].
- **The end title.** The paper says it is "probably hidden in an unsegmented area" "near this revealed text" (p. 12) [paper]. See section C.

### What the paper does NOT state
I checked this by reading every page and by searching the text layer. The paper does not give:
- winding or wrap numbers;
- a distance from the centre or umbilicus;
- whether the columns are in the inner or outer part of the roll;
- the reading direction relative to the spiral;
- whether the end of the sheet or the umbilicus was reached;
- how far segmentation extends beyond col. -1.

The only wording about wraps is "a previous circumference", in the discussion of a possible "virtual sottoposto" (p. 14; see D). For the roll's history and imaging, the paper refers to Nicolardi et al. 2024, ZPE 229, esp. pp. 6 f. (p. 9 n. 2).

### What the figures show at and after col. -1 [fig-measure]
The measurements are from Fig. 8; the outline is the same in Figs. 1 and 9.
- **Height of the col. -1 zone.** Only the upper part of the col. -1 zone is imaged. The lower edge of the strip steps up exactly at the intercolumn between cols. -2 and -1.
  - Cols. -11 to -2 are imaged down to about line 18-19.
  - The col. -1 zone is imaged only down to about the level of line 11-12 of the neighbouring columns. That is about 6-7 line-heights (~40-45 mm) less.
- **Blank area below col. -1.** The text of col. -1 fills the top 7 line positions. Below its last line there is ~30-38 mm (~4-5 line pitches) of blank papyrus before the imaged area stops.
- **Blank area to the right of col. -1.** The strip continues only ~12-14 mm to the right of col. -1's text before it ends. That is less than one 17 mm intercolumn.
- **What this means.** The published image shows nothing beyond about one intercolumn after col. -1, and nothing below about line 12 in the col. -1 zone. No writing is visible in the imaged blank area after or below col. -1.
- **Right end of the strip.** The paper does not say whether the right end is a segmentation limit or a physical edge. Its outline is irregular.

### Upper-edge scallops as a guide to turn size [fig-measure, with inference]
- **Basis in the paper.**
  - The authors attribute the strip's "evident wavy outline" to material damage to the roll, "which is taller on one side than on the other" (p. 10) [paper].
  - n. 6 calls wavy outlines "important material features for the reconstruction of papyrus rolls", citing Assante 2010 [paper].
- **Inference (mine).** A roll that is taller on one side should make the upper edge rise and fall once per turn. The spacing from crest to crest along the unwrapped strip should then roughly equal the perimeter of each turn.
- **Measured crest spacing along the upper edge of Fig. 8** (200-dpi px, converted at 3.3-3.5 px/mm):

  | Row of Fig. 8 | Columns | Crests | Spacing (px) | Spacing (mm) |
  |---|---|---|---|---|
  | 1 | line-ends of -16, then -15 to -11 | 7 full | ~200-210 falling to ~177 | ~60 falling to ~51 |
  | 2 | -10 to -6 | 9 | ~174 falling to ~136-140 | ~50 falling to ~39 |
  | 3 | -5 to -1 | 14 | ~123 falling to ~70 | ~37 falling to ~20 |

  - In the col. -1 zone itself the spacings are ~78, ~84 and ~70 px, about 20-25 mm.
  - The spacing falls smoothly across all three rows, by about 4-4.5 px (1.2-1.4 mm) per crest, with no jump at the row breaks.
  - There are about 30 crests in all along the ~1.2 m strip.
- **Implication, if one crest equals one turn.**
  - The strip moves inward as the text advances. Col. -16 lies on turns of ~55-60 mm perimeter, and col. -1 on turns of ~20-25 mm.
  - So the last column is nearer the roll's centre than col. -16, and anything after col. -1 would lie further inward still.
  - Near col. -1, one 62 mm column spans about 3 turns.
- **Rough extrapolation (speculative; nothing in the paper).** Suppose the perimeter keeps shrinking by ~1.2-1.4 mm per turn from ~20-21 mm. It would reach zero after ~16-17 more turns. That space could hold at most ~0.17-0.18 m (about 2 column pitches) of further papyrus. Treat this only as an upper bound, since the core may be hollow or crushed.
- **Caveats.**
  - The Grand Prize (GP) image is a flattened rendering of a crushed roll.
  - The authors warn that measurements reflect the roll's crumpled, shrunken shape (n. 5).
  - The crests were detected on a compressed journal figure.
  - One crest per turn is an assumption.

## B. Segments, data, people and dates

- **Scan data.** Acquired in 2019 by EduceLab (University of Kentucky) (p. 9) [paper]. The First Letters Prize (FLP) and GP images come from the same scan data (p. 9).
- **Not given anywhere in the paper:**
  - segment IDs, scan IDs or dataset names;
  - voxel size or resolution;
  - beam energy or facility;
  - any named software, tool or pipeline.
- **Method, as described (p. 15).** The AI inspects "tiny 3D regions of micro-CT data" for signals of ink. It gives "verdicts on the presence of ink in regions too small to contain a complete letter". Ink strokes become visible by aggregating many such regions.
  - None of the evaluated submissions used an algorithm trained to find or detect letterforms. The authors call this a "valuable guarantee of authenticity and objectivity".
- **Who segmented.** The paper does not say who segmented the GP strip. It credits Ben Kyles and David Josey of the Segmentation Team, and Stephen Parsons of the technical team, for the measurements (n. 5).
- **Prize teams and contestants.**
  - GP winners, announced 5 Feb 2024: Youssef Nader, Luke Farritor and Julian Schilliger (p. 9).
  - FLP, October 2023: Farritor first, Nader second (pp. 9-10).
  - GP runners-up (unnumbered note, p. 9): Shao-Qian Mah; Elian Rafael Dal Prá, Sean Johnson, Leonardo Scabini, Raí Fernando Dal Prá, João Vitor Brentigani Torezan, Daniel Baldin Franceschini, Bruno Pereira Kellm, Marcelo Soccol Gris and Odemir Martinez Bruno; Louis Schlessinger and Arefeh Sherafati.
  - Nicola Bodill, a contestant, shared preliminary labelling results. The authors consulted them under the Vesuvius Challenge Data Agreement. They had "not yet been technically evaluated" but are called "a promising first step towards improvement over the Grand Prize images" (p. 9).
- **Acknowledged.** W. Brent Seales, Nat Friedman, Daniel Gross, Stephen Parsons, the EduceLab team and the Vesuvius Challenge team (p. 9). The authors served as the Vesuvius Challenge "2023 Papyrology team".
- **Owner of the roll.** Académie des Inscriptions et Belles-Lettres, Institut de France (p. 9). Figure credit: "© Vesuvius Challenge-Académie des Inscriptions et Belles Lettres, Institut de France, Paris".
- **Column assignments among the authors (p. 9):**
  - Nicolardi: introduction and cols. -16, -15, -10, -5.
  - Fowler: cols. -14, -9, -4.
  - Del Mastro: cols. -13, -12, -11.
  - Delattre: cols. -8, -7, -6.
  - Janko: **cols. -3, -2, -1**.
  - The final text was a joint effort.
- **Evaluation of the GP submissions (p. 12).**
  - Twelve submissions were compared, and some teams submitted several images.
  - The criteria were "general readability and presence of noise".
  - Each author made an independent preliminary transcription.
  - The first-ranked submission had "much higher readability", but comparing different images of the same strip proved useful.
- **Dates.**
  - 2019: scan.
  - October 2023: FLP.
  - 5 Feb 2024: GP announced.
  - 5 June 2024: first Title Prize launched on PHerc.Paris. 4 (p. 12).
  - The paper describes itself as written "after only a few months of work" (p. 11).
- **Areas said to be unrevealed or still to explore:**
  1. The lower 5.38 cm of the roll's height along the whole strip. If that band has no lower margin, it holds an estimated 8/9 more lines (p. 10) [paper].
  2. "An unsegmented area" near the revealed text, where the end title probably is (p. 12) [paper].
  3. The columns before col. -16 (n. 4) [paper].
  4. The col. -1 zone below about the line 11-12 level, and anything more than ~12-14 mm to the right of col. -1's text [fig-measure].
  5. Cols. -16 to -12, which are imaged only to about line 12-13; the edition has 12-13 lines for cols. -15 to -12. The lower edge of the strip steps down at the -12/-11 intercolumn, and from col. -11 to col. -2 the columns reach line 18-19 [fig-measure + edition].
- **Web links in the notes.** https://scrollprize.org/grandprize (n. 1); https://scrollprize.org/2024_prizes, on the 2024 Vesuvius Challenge "ambitious goal" (n. 10).

## C. What the paper says about the title

- **The only explicit statement (p. 12):** "Near this revealed text, the end title of the work is probably hidden in an unsegmented area. On June 5, 2024, the Vesuvius Challenge team launched the first Title Prize on PHerc.Paris. 4, and we expect submissions in the coming months."
- **Not stated:**
  - exactly where the title is expected (below col. -1, in the blank after it, or in the lower band);
  - why it is expected there;
  - what has already been searched;
  - what imaging or segmentation would be needed.
- **Not mentioned at all:** initial titles, sillyboi (title tags), stichometry, coronis, protokollon or eschatokollion, and the umbilicus.
- **The only other mention of paratext (p. 14).** Traces in the intercolumn between cols. -12 and -11 "could be part of a scribal correction or intervention – if not some other paratextual element". This is not a title. See D.
- **Layout facts that bear on a title search:**
  - Col. -1 is the last column of text. Its text ends at line 7, on a short line of about 11 letters; a full line has about 15-16 [edition].
  - The edition prints no lacuna sign below col. -1, so the ending is treated as complete. Every other column ends with a lacuna sign [edition].
  - As far as it is imaged, the papyrus below col. -1's last line (~30-38 mm) and to its right (~12-14 mm) is blank. **No title is visible in any published image** [fig-measure].
  - If the upper-edge inference in A holds, anything following col. -1 lies on inner turns of ≤ ~20-25 mm perimeter [fig-measure, inference].

## D. Damage and preservation

- **Upper margin.** Lost. Segmentation "has reached the upper edge of the papyrus" (p. 10) [paper].
  - Every column in the edition begins with a lacuna sign, and line 1 is often partly lost [edition].
  - Letters lost at line 1: col. -15, about 10; col. -11, about 10; col. -10, about 9; col. -9, about 8; col. -7, about 7; col. -6, about 8 [edition].
- **Wavy outline.** It comes from material damage: the roll is "taller on one side than on the other" (p. 10) [paper].
  - In the FLP images, where segmentation had not yet reached the upper edge, the same feature came from "a digital damage" (n. 6, citing Nicolardi et al. 2024, 8).
  - Wavy outlines are "extremely frequent" in mechanically unrolled Herculaneum papyri (n. 6).
- **Lower band.** 5.38 cm of the roll's height is not yet revealed (p. 10) [paper].
- **Faint areas (p. 12).** "Several lines in the revealed portion still need to be fine-tuned, as the script often appears too faint to be read, possibly due to imperfect identification of the surface of an individual papyrus sheet within the scan data (segmentation)" [paper].
- **Segmentation imperfection versus hole (p. 14, Fig. 5).** At col. -14, 6 (FLP l. 5), the FLP image showed a small grey rectangle at the top left of a letter. The authors judged it "more likely to be a segmentation imperfection rather than a small hole". The GP image shows the letter complete [paper].
- **Possible layer mixing ("virtual sottoposto") (p. 14, Fig. 7).**
  - Between cols. -12 and -11, "particularly from l. 13 onwards", the GP images show something in the intercolumn, where no ink is expected. To the left of l. 17 it could suggest a letter.
  - These traces are not transcribed. The authors offer several explanations: a scribal correction or intervention, another paratextual element, noise, or the segmentation having "inadvertently isolated a part of a previous circumference, as in a «virtual sottoposto»" [paper].
  - [fig-measure] This spot coincides with the step in the strip's lower edge (col. -12 is imaged to l. 13, col. -11 to l. 18) and with a thin white vertical seam in Fig. 8, row 1. This is an observation only.
- **AI artefacts documented (pp. 13-14; Figs. 3, 4, 6)** [paper]:
  - At col. -15, 5, a sinusoidal stroke through a letter, likely AI "noise accentuation". It fades in the less legible GP submissions.
  - At col. -14, 5, a rounded stroke suggesting a correction. It is absent in GP.
  - At col. -15, 4, an oblique tick like a punctuation mark. It is absent in GP, and the GP image shows there was no room for punctuation.
  - Earlier, "AI suggested traces that were impossible", and noise was "amplified by the AI, resulting in hallucinations" (p. 13; n. 15, citing Nicolardi et al. 2024, 8 f.).
- **Seams in Fig. 8 [fig-measure; not discussed by the authors].** There are thin white vertical breaks at about the -14/-13 intercolumn and the -12/-11 intercolumn (row 1), and near the left edge of col. -8 (row 2). The paper does not say whether they are joins between separately flattened pieces or cracks.
- **Col. -16.** Only the line-ends of 7 lines survive, plus "traces at the line ends" in ll. 8-12 (apparatus: "ll. 8-12 vestigia in linearum fine", p. 15) [edition].
- **The last three columns [edition layout signs only; no readings]:**
  - **Col. -3 (18 lines).**
    - Line 1 has lost letters in brackets.
    - There are long runs of unread letters (dots) in ll. 9-10 and 12-16.
    - There may be a supra-lineam correction at ll. 13 f. (n. 9).
  - **Col. -2 (18 lines).**
    - Line 1 is partly lost, and there is an unread run in l. 9.
    - Line 18 starts with a bracket, and the column ends with a lacuna sign. The text continues into the unrevealed lower band.
    - The apparatus note "ante 1" at col. -1 proposes letters ending the previous column, so the unrevealed bottom of col. -2 runs on into col. -1, l. 1.
  - **Col. -1 (7 lines).**
    - Line 1 is not transcribed; the first printed line is numbered 2. This matches the citations "col. -1, 4 f." and "col. -1, 7".
    - In Fig. 8 the first visible line lies on the damaged wavy upper edge and is faint.
    - Lines 2-7 are transcribed with only a few uncertain letters, and the text ends at l. 7.
    - The paper does not discuss any damage in the blank area after col. -1.
- **Other damage.** Col. -4, ll. 13-15 are largely unread [edition]. In general, runs of unread letters cluster in the middle and lower lines of many columns [edition]. The paper does not discuss holes, crushing or other damage in the area after col. -1.

## E. Writing and layout details useful for detection

- **Date of the script.** Between the 1st century BC and the 1st century AD (p. 10; parallels in Nicolardi et al. 2024, 7) [paper].
- **Column format (p. 10)** [paper]:
  - The layout is "regular and organic".
  - Columns are about **62 mm** wide.
  - Intercolumns are about **17 mm**.
  - The average pitch from one column to the next is **79 mm**.
- **Lines per column.**
  - The paper says up to 18 consecutive lines are revealed (p. 10) [paper].
  - The edition numbers 19 lines in cols. -9 and -6; 18 in cols. -11, -10, -8, -7, -5, -4, -3 and -2; 12-13 in cols. -15 to -12; and 7 in col. -1 [edition].
- **Line pitch.** Not stated.
  - [derived] About 6.3 mm (11.37 cm ÷ 18). The paper's 8/9 lines in 5.38 cm implies ~6.0-6.7 mm.
  - [fig-measure] About 6-7 mm.
- **Letters per line.** A full line has a median of about **15-16 letters** [edition count]. [derived] That gives a letter pitch of about 4 mm across a 62 mm column.
- **Letter size and stroke width.** Not stated in the paper.
- **Punctuation signs** [paper]: paragraphos, diple, and asteriskos "used as a filler at the end of a line" (p. 10, Fig. 2). Positions in the edition [edition]:
  - diple at the left of col. -15, l. 3;
  - paragraphos at the left of col. -15, l. 4; col. -14, l. 2; col. -7, l. 13; col. -6, l. 14;
  - asteriskos at the end of col. -12, l. 7;
  - none printed in cols. -3 to -1.
  - Punctuation added by the editors ("interpunxit") is modern and not physical.
- **Possible blank space.** Within col. -9, l. 7 (apparatus: "spatium vacuum ... fortasse videtur") [edition].
- **Corrections** [paper, edition]:
  - At col. -4, 3, the scribe replaces a misplaced letter by writing the correct one *supra lineam* (p. 11).
  - There is possibly a similar case at col. -3, 13 f. (n. 9).
  - The edition also prints a letter written above the line at col. -9, 5.
- **Word division.** Words are often divided at line ends, and a word also runs from col. -2 into col. -1 [edition].
- **End of the text.** Col. -1 ends at l. 7 on a short line (~11 letters against ~15-16), with no lacuna sign after it. The rest of col. -1 is blank as far as it is imaged [edition, fig-measure].
- **Decoration.** None mentioned: no coronis and no ornament.
- **How ink looks in the images:**
  - GP image (Fig. 8): greyscale, with ink as dark or black strokes on a light-grey speckled ground and noise as scattered dark speckle.
  - FLP images (Figs. 3-6): darker grey ground with blotchier strokes.
  - Photo-realistic rendering (Fig. 9): black letters on brown-ochre papyrus.
  - The paper says the script "often appears too faint to be read" in several lines, and that "different algorithms may produce different levels of noise" (p. 12) [paper].
  - [fig-measure] In col. -1, ll. 3-7 are among the darkest and clearest lines in Fig. 8, while l. 2 is faint on the upper edge.
- **A possible mark at the start of col. -1, l. 7 [fig-measure; unverified].** In Fig. 8 the first letter of this line shows a horizontal stroke at baseline level that reaches slightly into the left margin. It may be part of the letter. The edition prints no paragraphos there, and the figure's resolution is too low to decide.

## G. The authors' suggestions for future work and imaging

- **Iterative ink detection (unnumbered note, p. 9).** "Improving the ink detection will increasingly rely on a tightened collaboration between papyrology and machine learning, through which the ink predictions are refined based on papyrological interpretation before being used again as labeled training data in an iterative process."
- **Bodill's labelling (p. 9).** Nicola Bodill's preliminary labelling is described as a promising step beyond the GP images, still to be evaluated technically.
- **The title (p. 12).** The Title Prize was launched on 5 June 2024, and the authors "expect submissions in the coming months". They say the end title is probably in an unsegmented area near the revealed text.
- **The lower band (p. 10).** The unrevealed lower 5.38 cm could still yield 8/9 lines.
- **Earlier columns (n. 4).** The columns before col. -16 are expected to be found "soon".
- **Faint lines (p. 12).** These need "fine-tuning". Their faintness is linked to imperfect identification of the sheet surface in segmentation.
- **Provisional status (p. 12).** Transcriptions and images are provisional, and the images "will be gradually replaced, or at least complemented, by newer, more precise, and more complete versions".
- **Method (pp. 12-13, 15).** Compare several independent renderings of the same strip. This helped separate real ink from AI noise and hallucinations. Avoid algorithms trained to detect letterforms, for the sake of authenticity. Examining the original under a microscope "is not – and will never be – possible", so AI-enabled images are the only way to see the ink.
- **Intercolumn traces (p. 14).** For the -12/-11 intercolumn, "we are confident that future progress will help us clarify this point".
